Case study by Austin Way

Circuit: a Minecraft server that matched players by who they’d get along with

Austin founded Circuit, also called Mine Circuit, as a Minecraft minigame server with one question behind it: if matchmaking put you with people you’d actually get along with, instead of only people at your skill level, would you keep coming back? The server started from zero players on Minehut, shipped 20 custom minigames, and reached 200,000 players. Circuit has since shut down, and Austin has moved from games into edtech.

01 The idea

Games keep players with loot. Circuit bet on friends.

Most online games build teams with skill-based matchmaking: find players with a similar rating and a good connection, and get them into a game quickly. That makes fair matches, but it treats your teammates as interchangeable. Nobody asks whether you’d want to play with those people again.

To keep players around, games lean on rewards: points, loot boxes, achievements. Reward loops wear thin as players get used to them, and every update has to be bigger than the last to feel the same. Austin’s argument was that games should shift weight from rewards to relationships, collaboration and community, because people come back for their friends long after the loot stops being exciting.

If matchmaking optimized for social fit instead of only skill, would players come back more often?

The question Circuit was built to test

Testing that inside an existing game requires a studio’s player base. A Minecraft server removes the barrier: whoever runs the server owns the whole loop, from the lobby to who plays with whom to whether players come back the next day. So instead of a survey or a prototype, Austin built a server and opened it to real players.

02 What Circuit was

A full minigame server, not a lab.

For the experiment to mean anything, players had to want to be there. Circuit was a complete minigame network: a hand-built lobby, around 20 custom games, progression, pets, a battlepass and voice chat, built on about 20,000 lines of custom code. A session looked like this.

  1. Arrive in the lobby

    Players spawned into a hand-built hub of themed builds, the starting point for every game.

  2. Vote on the next game

    A voting menu listed the games on offer. Hovering one showed its average star rating, your own rating and how many players had already voted for it, so groups chose together what to play.

  3. Play a round

    The games were all custom. In Powers of Fortune everyone gets a new power every 20 seconds and the last player alive wins. In the Random Kits round in the footage, twelve players share an arena with three lives each. Others included Pillar of Chaos, Team Platform and a multi-stage boss fight against the Corrupted Evoker.

  4. Progress between rounds

    Players carried a level and XP bar, a 150-level battlepass and a coin balance. Coins went into pets, which players hatched, named and upgraded, and which carried perks such as a gold boost.

  5. Talk to the people near you

    A Circuit-branded browser client carried in-game music and proximity voice chat, so players within 45 blocks of each other could talk, with a mute key for privacy.

Around that loop sat the experiment: deciding who ended up playing together. The games, rewards and voice chat gave players reasons to show up. The matchmaker was there to test whether the people they met would make them stay.

    04 How matchmaking worked

    Same players, a different question.

    A conventional matchmaker asks whether a group of players is evenly matched. Circuit asked whether a group would get along. Skill still mattered, since a hopeless match isn’t fun for anyone, but it stopped being the only thing the matchmaker optimized for.

    Skill-based matchmaking compared with Circuit’s social-cue matchmaking
    Aspect Skill-based matchmaking Circuit
    The question Are these players evenly matched? Will these players get along?
    What it weighs Rating, connection quality, time in queue Social cues about how someone plays with other people, alongside skill
    Between sessions A new set of strangers each game Groups that worked are brought back together
    Success looks like A close game A player who comes back

    Three design principles

    1. Fast first, then fit.

      A new player needs a game right away, so early matches favor speed. As a player returns, the matchmaker can afford to care less about queue time and more about who they’re grouped with.

      First session: speed and skillReturning player: social fit

    2. Keep good groups together.

      Familiarity builds friendship. When a group plays well together, putting the same people back in a game is worth more than a marginally better-balanced lobby of strangers.

    3. Shared struggle beats perfect balance.

      Easy wins are forgettable. Teams bond when they have to work together to get through something hard, like a boss with several stages, so a good match isn’t always a perfectly fair one.

    05 Growth

    From zero players on Minehut to 200,000.

    Circuit launched on Minehut, a Minecraft server host with a public list of servers that players browse to find somewhere to play. Austin tracked weekly unique users (players who joined at least once in a week) in weekly progress reports. The first stretch looked like this.

    • Top of the Minehut charts

      In the week it passed 1,000 weekly users, Circuit topped the Minehut charts with 22 players online at once.

    • A community off the server

      The Circuit Discord grew from about 100 members to over 300 over the same stretch.

    • A streamer on the server

      A streamer with more than 16,000 followers appeared on Circuit and spoke about its team dynamics.

    • Top 1% of servers

      By the Week 9 milestone, Circuit ranked in the top 1% of Minecraft servers and had logged 20,000 hours of player time.

    Weekly users measure how many people show up in a given week. Over the life of the server, Circuit reached 200,000 players in total.

    Circuit grew to 200,000 players, enough to see the idea work outside a slide deck. Players placed by the social matchmaker were roughly one-third more likely to come back.

    How to read this. The return-rate figure is Austin’s reading of the server’s player data. It isn’t peer-reviewed, and it shows that matched players came back more, not exactly why.

    Separate test, not the Minecraft server data

    The Circuit algorithm, tested on its own

    A separate test of the Circuit algorithm placed participants in groups of six for a two-hour session. After 30 days, 83% of participants reported they were still talking online. That is a different study with a different group of people, and it isn’t part of the 200,000-player server figures above.

    07 What happened next

    One more game, then a different path.

    After the Minecraft server, Austin started a second game, this time on Roblox, and built it all the way up to release. Around then, Roblox’s public reputation collapsed under accusations that it was unsafe for kids, including claims that it had become a platform for pedophiles. With that hanging over the platform, he didn’t feel he could release the game.

    That was roughly when he stepped away from games. In his words: “I’m done.” Around the same time he had started working in edtech, and he realized that was what he actually wanted to do with his life. That is where he is now. This page is the record of the games chapter that came before it.

    08 Lessons and status

    What the project showed.

    1. Social fit is its own retention lever.

      Skill balance and social fit are different variables. Optimizing only for the first leaves the second to chance.

    2. Content gets players in; people are the reason to stay.

      Twenty minigames, pets and a battlepass gave players reasons to try Circuit. The bet was that who they met there would decide whether they came back.

    3. The first match and the tenth need different goals.

      New players need a game now. Returning players can wait a little longer for a better group.

    4. A live test beats a hypothetical.

      Running a real server, from zero players on Minehut to 200,000, turned an opinion about matchmaking into behavior that could be observed.

    Why it matters

    Games spend heavily on new content to keep players around, and content runs out. A community doesn’t: the people you play with are content that keeps renewing itself. If matchmaking can help form those communities on purpose, retention stops depending on a bigger update each season. The same idea reaches beyond games: in the Rat Park experiments of the late 1970s, rats given company and space to play took far less morphine than rats kept alone.

    It also raises a harder question that Circuit leaves open: if a system can choose your teammates well, how much of your social circle should it choose?

    Current status

    Circuit is shut down, and the Roblox game that followed it was never released. There is no server to join, no game to play and no signup. Austin now works in edtech; this site is an archive of the Circuit experiment and what it found.
